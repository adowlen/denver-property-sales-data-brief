# What Denver actually sold for: 105,585 market sales since 2020

Machine Readable - 2026-10-16

**One-line summary:** Denver's median market sale price rose from $450,000 in 2020 to $595,000 in 2022 and has held between $525,000 and $550,000 since, across 105,585 market sales recorded in the assessor's sales and transfers ledger from 2020 through the October 15, 2026 snapshot.

## TL;DR

- The median market sale price rose 32 percent from 2020 ($450,000) to a 2022 peak ($595,000), then held in a narrow band: $550,000 in 2023, $540,000 in 2024, $525,000 in 2025. All figures are nominal dollars, as recorded.
- Sales volume peaked a year before prices did: 22,120 market sales in 2021, falling 45 percent to 12,168 in 2023 and running at about 13,000 in 2024-2025. Residential new-building permits followed the same arc (2,343 in 2021 to 1,309 in 2025).
- The share of market sales at or under $500,000 fell from 57.2 percent in 2020 to 38.6 percent in 2022, then recovered partway to 47.4 percent in 2025.
- Half of what sold was a single-family house (52.4 percent of market sales pooled 2020-2026, 2026 partial, median $580,000). Condominiums were 23.5 percent (median $330,000) and rowhouses 16.9 percent (median $577,495).
- The median single-family sale in Cherry Creek ($2,195,000) was 6.6 times the median in Globeville ($335,000). Between 2020-21 and 2024-25, the citywide single-family median rose 11.2 percent, with a range from -10.4 percent (Lincoln Park) to +35.6 percent (Southmoor Park) across the 68 neighborhoods with enough sales to measure.
- On the parcel record, 3,297 Denver parcels carry a demolition dated between January 1, 2020 and October 12, 2026, and 70.8 percent of them later received a new-building permit - a floor, since recent demolitions have had less time to be followed by one (Finding 5). The 611 parcels that completed a full recorded cycle - a market purchase before the demolition, then a market sale after the rebuild permit - show a median purchase of $650,000 and a median post-permit sale of $2,299,525; the median difference between the two deeds is $1,635,000, before construction and financing costs, over a median of 28.3 months.

## What we used

Denver's assessor publishes a single ledger of Real Property Sales and Transfers: every recorded transfer of ownership, 313,361 rows with sale years 2015-2026 in the October 15, 2026 snapshot. (The catalog description says the file reaches back to 2010; the earliest sale year actually in the data is 2015, and nothing in this brief reaches before 2020.) The ledger is a transfers file, not a sales file: 56,031 rows (17.9 percent) record a price of zero or no price at all - quit claim deeds between family members, estate transfers, death certificates, parcel splits, correction deeds.

This brief counts a **market sale** as a transfer by warranty deed (WD) or special warranty deed (SW) with a recorded price above zero - the two deed types that behave like arm's-length sales. That filter gives 105,585 parcel rows for 2020 and later, covering 98,374 distinct reception numbers plus one row with none (98,375 deal records; one transaction can span several parcels, and the source repeats its price on each parcel row; prices are never summed here, and every median is computed on parcel rows unless a figure says otherwise). The definition is imperfect in both directions and we say so wherever it matters: some warranty-deed transfers are still related-party, and the occasional quit claim is a genuine sale. There is no qualified-sale flag in the public file, so this filter is the substitute.

Sale dates are built from the ledger's sale year and month/day fields. The recording (reception) date is used only to measure lag: the median deed was recorded 3 days after its sale date, and 96.7 percent within 30 days. (The reception field is itself imperfect: 537 recorded deeds show a reception date before their sale date and 4 have none. The lag figures are computed over the 105,581 market sales that carry a reception date, negative lags count outside the 30-day window, and the median is unaffected.) The published table loads in batches, and in this snapshot sales are substantially complete only through August 31, 2026. September 2026 shows 207 recorded sales against 1,099 in September 2025; that gap is recording lag, not a market event, and 2026 is labeled partial wherever it appears, including in pooled totals. One recorded sale carries a December 30, 2026 sale date - after the snapshot itself - and is excluded from every dated figure; the dated analysis covers 105,584 sales.

Neighborhoods are the city's 78 statistical neighborhoods, assigned through the parcel record: each sale keys to a current parcel by its assessor schedule number, and the parcel carries its statistical neighborhood. 98.54 percent of sales rows carrying a schedule number (all but one of 313,361) key to a parcel that exists today, and 103,823 of the 105,584 dated market sales (98.3 percent) receive a neighborhood. The unassigned remainder are sales of parcels since retired by splits and mergers. The assessor's own neighborhood field in the ledger is a different geography and is not used. As a check on the join itself: the parcels file carries the assessor's own last-transfer summary per parcel, produced by a different assessor product than the sales ledger, and for the 152,181 parcels whose last-transfer reception number matches a ledger row on a numeric key the two records agree exactly on price for 99.85 percent, and on sale year for 99.91 percent. The 221 disagreements are concentrated in exactly the ambiguous rows this brief excludes: in 43.9 percent of them the ledger records a price of zero where the parcels summary carries one.

Prices are joined to the permit, demolition, and parcel layers from our two previous briefs for Finding 5. All prices are nominal dollars as recorded; nothing is inflation-adjusted, and every median describes what sold in that period - the mix of properties that changed hands - not a repeat-sales price index.

## Finding 1: Volume peaked in 2021, prices in 2022, and both then flattened

Market sales rose from 19,787 in 2020 to 22,120 in 2021, then fell to 16,829 in 2022 and 12,168 in 2023 - a 45.0 percent drop from the peak in two years. Volume has been flat since: 13,100 in 2024 and 12,852 in 2025. The median price moved a year behind volume: $450,000 in 2020, $525,000 in 2021, a peak of $595,000 in 2022, then $550,000 (2023), $540,000 (2024), and $525,000 (2025). The 2025 median sits 16.7 percent above 2020 and 11.8 percent below the 2022 peak, in nominal dollars.

New-building permits in the residential permit layer, from our October 15 brief, trace the same arc at smaller scale: 2,343 permits in 2021, 2,002 in 2022, 1,887 in 2023, 1,330 in 2024, 1,309 in 2025. Construction permitting and existing-home sales peaked in the same year and fell by similar shares (-44.1 percent for permits, 2021 to 2025, against -41.9 percent for market sales). The two series come from different records - the assessor's ledger and the permit system - and they peak in the same year.

## Finding 2: The under-$500,000 share fell sharply in 2022 and has only partly returned

In 2020, 57.2 percent of market sales closed at or under $500,000. By 2022 that share was 38.6 percent: the bottom band (at or under $250,000) shrank from 19.7 percent of sales to 11.8 percent, while sales above $1.5 million rose from 6.2 percent to 12.9 percent of the total. Since then the distribution has moved partway back: 47.4 percent of 2025 sales were at or under $500,000, and the over-$1.5 million share eased to 9.8 percent. The recovery is entirely a bottom-band recovery: the under-$250,000 band refilled past its 2020 share (21.2 percent in 2025 against 19.7 percent in 2020), while the $250,000-$500,000 band has not recovered at all - 37.6 percent of sales in 2020, 26.1 percent in 2025. The median tells the same story in one number (Finding 1), but the bands show where the movement happened: the 2022 market did not just shift up, it thinned out at the bottom.

## Finding 3: Half of what sold was a single-family house; a quarter was a condominium

Of the 105,584 market sales in the dated analysis (2020-2026, 2026 partial), single-family residential properties (the assessor's SFR classes) account for 55,306 (52.4 percent), at a median of $580,000. Condominiums account for 24,764 (23.5 percent) at a median of $330,000, and rowhouses for 17,888 (16.9 percent) at a median of $577,495. The rest is small in count and large in price: duplexes, triplexes, and 4-to-8-unit buildings, 1,312 sales at a median of $650,000; larger apartment properties, 670 sales at a median of $4,999,500; commercial and industrial properties, 3,802 sales at a median of $2,100,000; and vacant land, 1,321 sales at a median of $200,000. A further 521 sales (0.5 percent) fall outside these classes or carry no class, at a median of $1,010,000.

This composition is why every neighborhood median in this brief needs its basis stated. A neighborhood where most sales are condominiums will post a low median even if its houses are expensive, and a neighborhood's median can move because the mix of what sold changed, not because any property's value did. Finding 4 therefore reports neighborhood levels and movements on the single-family basis, where the comparison is like for like.

## Finding 4: Single-family medians run from $335,000 to $2,195,000 across neighborhoods

Pooling 2020-2026 (2026 partial), and ranking neighborhoods with at least 100 pooled single-family sales, the highest median single-family sale prices are in Cherry Creek ($2,195,000, 159 sales), Country Club ($1,625,000, 429), Belcaro ($1,483,415, 703), Hilltop ($1,400,000, 1,114), and Washington Park ($1,355,820, 1,282). The lowest are in Globeville ($335,000, 282 sales), Elyria Swansea ($336,663, 346), Westwood ($350,000, 990), Valverde ($375,000, 282), and Barnum ($378,000, 607). The Cherry Creek median is 6.6 times the Globeville median. The 100-sale rule excludes Sun Valley from this list: its 9 single-family sales carry a median of $370,000, which would otherwise rank fourth-lowest on a count too small to rank.

Movement between 2020-21 and 2024-25 (partial-2026 sales are in neither period), for the 68 neighborhoods with at least 30 single-family sales in both periods: the citywide single-family median rose 11.2 percent, from $535,000 to $595,000. The largest increases are Southmoor Park (+35.6 percent, $763,000 to $1,035,000, on 88 and 65 sales), Belcaro (+31.2 percent, $1,225,000 to $1,607,500, on 281 and 170), Cole (+27.0 percent, $518,200 to $658,000, on 184 and 99), Windsor (+26.1 percent, $575,000 to $725,000, on 61 and 36), Cory - Merrill (+25.9 percent, $737,500 to $928,750, on 354 and 252), and Marston (+25.8 percent, $485,000 to $610,000, on 270 and 198). Four neighborhoods recorded lower single-family medians in 2024-25 than in 2020-21: Lincoln Park (-10.4 percent, $512,000 to $458,750, on 109 and 58 sales), University Park (-9.8 percent, $1,100,000 to $992,500, on 317 and 216), Cheesman Park (-7.5 percent, $992,000 to $917,500, on 92 and 56), and Five Points (-6.3 percent, $654,500 to $613,500, on 180 and 116). Country Club (+1.6 percent, on 167 and 102 sales), Montclair (+4.2 percent, on 369 and 225), and Central Park (+4.9 percent, on 1,484 and 781 - the city's largest sale counts) were close to flat.

One neighborhood shows why the basis matters. In Goldsmith, the all-sales median rose 115.8 percent between the two periods ($201,250 to $434,200) - because low-priced condominiums dominated its 2020-21 sales and houses dominated later. On the single-family basis, Goldsmith's median rose 20.3 percent ($557,000 to $670,000), in line with the city. The full neighborhood table, on both bases and with each neighborhood's under-$500,000 shares, is in data/neighborhood_sales.csv; rows whose change percentages rest on fewer than 30 sales in either period are flagged in that file and are not ranked anywhere in this brief.

## Finding 5: The teardown cycle, priced end to end

Joining the sales ledger to the demolition and construction permit layers by parcel: 3,297 parcels carry a first demolition permit dated between January 1, 2020 and October 12, 2026, and 2,335 of them (70.8 percent) later received a new-building permit on the same parcel record. Two cautions attach to that rate. First, the record shows permits, not construction: a permit is an authorization, and nothing in these layers certifies that a building was completed. Second, the rate is a floor: the 962 parcels not counted as rebuilt mix parcels that will never be rebuilt with parcels whose permits have not been issued yet. Demolitions from the window's final 12 months (460 parcels) show a 46.1 percent rebuilt share so far, against 74.8 percent for demolitions from earlier in the window. (The unit here is the parcel; our October 15 brief counted 3,837 demolition permits by street address. Permits and parcels are different units - one address can cover several parcels, and some address-matched permits do not key to a current parcel - so the two counts are not expected to agree, and the 70.8 percent is not the same measurement as that brief's 60.0 percent same-address follow rate.)

Of the 2,335 rebuilt parcels, 1,428 (61.2 percent) have a recorded market purchase before the demolition; the median such purchase was $690,638. 1,012 (43.3 percent) have a recorded market sale after the rebuild permit; the median first such sale was $1,875,000. Both shares are floors: purchases before 2015 are outside the ledger, purchases made by non-market transfer are outside the definition, and rebuilds that have not sold yet are invisible until they do. The buy and exit definitions use strict date ordering - the purchase must fall before the demolition date, the exit after the permit date - and the near misses are small: no recorded sale falls exactly on a parcel's demolition date, one falls exactly on a permit date, and 77 sales fall between a parcel's demolition and its rebuild permit and so count as neither a buy nor an exit.

The 611 parcels that completed both sides of the cycle inside the record - a market purchase before demolition and a market sale after the rebuild permit - are 99 percent single-family purchases (606 of 611). Their median purchase was $650,000, their median post-permit sale $2,299,525, and the median difference between the two deeds is $1,635,000 over a median of 28.3 months between deeds. That spread is gross: it is measured before demolition, construction, holding, and financing costs, and it is not a profit figure. What it measures is the size of the value step the teardown model has to pay for, at the median, in the city's own records. Two sensitivity checks bound the obvious distortions. Only 7 of the 611 paired parcels have a buy or sell deed that formed part of a multi-parcel transaction (where the source repeats the whole transaction price on each parcel's row); restricting the paired set to single-parcel deeds (604 parcels) moves the median spread to $1,637,601. And the paired purchases are recent relative to the demolition by construction of the cycle - the median purchase closed 7.6 months before the demolition and 91.2 percent closed within 36 months; restricting to those 557 parcels gives a median spread of $1,649,000.

The sales ledger also reads back against the net-change brief's outliers. Sun Valley recorded 78 demolition permits and no residential new-building permits in the window (October 15 brief, permit basis), and 64 market sales in the same period; Civic Center, also net-negative on residential permits, recorded 413 market sales. At the neighborhood level, then, both areas recorded sales activity across a window in which they recorded no residential construction permits - the sales and permit records measure different events, and neither contradicts the other. The three high-churn neighborhoods from that brief (Washington Park, Hilltop, Belcaro) are also three of the five most expensive single-family markets in this one, with pooled all-sales medians of $1,150,000, $1,300,000, and $1,147,500 respectively.

## What this might mean

One reading of these numbers is that Denver's market repriced once, sharply, in 2020-2022, and has since moved sideways at the new level: volume fell by nearly half and stayed down, the median gave back about half of its 2020-2022 gain from the peak ($70,000 of $145,000) and then held in a $525,000-$550,000 band for three years, and the under-$500,000 share recovered only partway. Under this reading, the 2022 distribution - thin at the bottom, heavy at the top - was the anomaly, and 2023-2025 is the settled market.

The strongest alternative explanation is composition and units. These are medians of what sold, in nominal dollars. If the mix of homes that sell shifts toward larger or renovated properties, the median rises without any individual home appreciating; if it shifts toward condominiums, the median falls the same way. A repeat-sales index, which this ledger could support but this brief does not build, would separate price movement from mix movement. And in inflation-adjusted terms the picture flattens further: consumer prices rose materially over 2020-2025, so the 2025 median's 16.7 percent nominal lead over 2020 is smaller in real terms; some neighborhoods whose nominal single-family medians rose least (Country Club +1.6 percent, Central Park +4.9 percent) plausibly fell in real terms, and Cheesman Park's fell outright in nominal terms (-7.5 percent). The volume finding does not depend on any of this - sales counts are counts - but every price-level comparison in this brief should be read with the mix and nominal-dollar caveats attached.

For the teardown figures, the alternative reading is selection: the 611 paired parcels are the cycles that completed inside one window, which favors faster projects and neighborhoods where rebuilds sell. The unpaired medians (purchase $690,638 over 1,428 parcels; post-permit sale $1,875,000 over 1,012) describe wider sets and a smaller value step. Both are printed so the reader can see the range rather than a single polished number.

## Who can act

- **Denver Assessor's Office (publisher of the sales and transfers ledger).** The lever: publish the deed-instrument codebook and a market-sale indicator alongside the file. Every downstream user of this dataset currently has to re-derive the warranty-deed filter used here and recover the 50-plus instrument codes from a second file's value labels; the office holds the definitions. This is a documentation change to an existing publication, the lowest cost class. The measurable outcome: whether the dataset's documentation names the instrument codes and the zero-price convention in a future release.
- **Denver City Council.** The lever: housing measures debated in the current budget cycle (public hearing October 26, 2026) can be scored against measured baselines from the city's own records - the share of market sales at or under $500,000 (57.2 percent in 2020, 47.4 percent in 2025) and the single-family median by neighborhood. The outcome that would show a measure working: those shares and medians moving in later snapshots of this same ledger.
- **Denver Department of Housing Stability.** The lever: the neighborhood table published with this brief carries each neighborhood's share of sales at or under $500,000 for 2020-21 and 2024-25 (75 neighborhoods with at least 30 sales in both periods). The share fell fastest in Bear Valley (81.2 to 52.1 percent), Capitol Hill (80.7 to 53.2 percent), and Harvey Park South (66.3 to 41.8 percent) - a ready-made watch list for where entry-level stock is thinning. The outcome measure: repeat this measurement on each new snapshot and report which neighborhoods' low-price shares recover.
- **Denver Community Planning and Development.** The lever: demolition and deconstruction policy debates now have parcel-basis baselines from the city's records: 70.8 percent of demolished parcels received a new-building permit in the window (a floor - demolitions from the final 12 months stand at 46.1 percent so far), and a median difference of $1,635,000 between the purchase and post-permit sale deeds on completed teardown cycles, before construction and financing costs. Any proposal in this area can be evaluated against whether those rates move in the following permit periods.

## Methods and caveats, short version

- Market sale means warranty or special warranty deed with a recorded price above zero. 56,031 transfers (17.9 percent of the ledger) record zero or no price and are excluded from every price figure; they remain transfers, and this brief does not call them sales.
- Prices are never summed across parcels. One transaction spanning several parcels repeats its price on each parcel row (7,210 extra rows among the 105,585 recorded parcel rows: 98,374 distinct reception numbers plus one row with none); medians are computed on parcel rows, and the distinct-transaction median for 2024 is $536,250 against $540,000 on the parcel-row basis.
- All prices are nominal dollars as recorded. Medians describe what sold, not a price index; composition changes move them (see the Goldsmith case in Finding 4). Band shares are rounded to one decimal; the at-or-under-$500,000 share is computed before rounding, so rounded components may sum 0.1 point off the printed share.
- The October 15, 2026 snapshot's sales are substantially complete through August 31, 2026. September 2026 (207 sales recorded, against 1,099 in September 2025) is a recording-lag partial, October 2026 holds 1 recorded sale, and one sale dated December 30, 2026 - after the snapshot - is excluded from every dated figure. 2026 is labeled partial wherever it appears, including in pooled totals. Movement periods are 2020-21 and 2024-25; partial-2026 sales are in neither.
- 1,761 dated market sales (1.7 percent) could not be assigned a statistical neighborhood because their parcels have since been retired by splits or mergers; neighborhood figures cover the remaining 103,823. Unassigned sales skew older within the window. Citywide medians are computed on the full dated population, assigned or not.
- No price-per-square-foot figures are published. The assessor's characteristics file describes the building standing today, not the one that sold, and after teardowns and remodels those are often different buildings; the layer was cut under our layering admission test rather than used decoratively.
- Nothing here is a finding about any individual property, buyer, or seller. Properties are counted, never named.
- Full methods checklist, the layering admission test, the pre-flight audit, and the independent verification ledger are in verification.md and sources.md in this repository.
